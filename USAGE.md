# USAGE — running the regime pipeline on your production data

This guide maps your production stack (the momentum/basis system you shared: `cleanLeg`, `tbaRollDF`, `dynamicHedgeAdj`, the `tbaswhedgeratio` model, `TSY_ROLLDAY`, `ROLL_ADJUST_DAY_LIST`) onto the pipeline's inputs, then explains how to read every output for the three regime questions: when the hedge relationship breaks, when the basis distribution shifts, and when the P&L slope flips from carry-earning to widening — and where each change comes from.

## 1. Producing the inputs from your stack

All files are daily. Collapse your intraday snapshots to one synchronized cutoff — the *same* timestamp for every leg (e.g. last quote at or before 15:00; on your `EARLYCLOSEDATES`, use the early-close time for all legs). Your current `addClose` takes each leg's last snapshot independently, which can misalign legs on halted or early-close days; a common cutoff removes that.

**The single most important warning: export RAW prices, not your adjusted `Mid`.** Your `cleanLeg` overwrites `Mid` with `Mid + cumAdj` (roll-adjusted). The pipeline performs its own roll adjustment from front/back prices, so feeding your adjusted series would double-adjust. Export the original `Price` from `queryHist` (or reconstruct as `Mid − cumAdj`).

| Pipeline file | From your stack |

On cleaning: prefer raw closes. Your `cleanLeg` outlier filter uses a full-sample `globalStd` capped at 0.3, so quotes more than ~1.05 points from the day's mean are deleted — on genuine crisis days that censors exactly the observations regime detection needs. If you must reuse cleaned data, spot-check known event days survived (March 2020, SVB week, big CPI prints).

On the hedge-ratio audit log: the historical intraday change times are unrecoverable from your current tables (the `EXACT_MODIFIED_TIME` lists cover only hardcoded dates plus the live session). Going forward, persist (date, modification datetime, old, new) per coupon whenever the update script detects a change; until then the timing-band table is your exposure measure.

## 2. Running

`pip install -r requirements.txt`, then `python run.py --synthetic` once — it must print 8/8 recovered planted events; this validates the environment. Then `python run.py --data-dir ./yourdata`. Outputs land in `./outputs`: `report.md` (read top to bottom), figures `fig1–fig7`, and CSVs for every intermediate (node betas, ratios, basis panel, coupon swaps, Kalman beta, drift components, attribution, timing band). Config knobs live in `config.py`; the defaults match the quant spec (60d window, 10bp grid, poly3 curve). With short history (<2 years) distrust the HMM and MS layers entirely and rely on the Kalman and PELT layers.

## 3. Reading the report — section by section

**Data integrity first.** CC extrapolation percentage (a convention change here manufactures breaks), Treasury splice gaps, and — before anything else — reconcile daily basis increments against your own `Mid_dynamicAdj` changes (see §5). Do not interpret regimes until reconciliation is clean.

**Variant A vs B and the horse race.** This tells you which procedure production actually runs. If production loads on A (the literal moneyness-anchored differencing), the composition effect applies: production ratios understate fixed-coupon duration, more when CC is mobile, and desk deviations above benchmark after selloffs are partially *corrections*, not discretion. If production loads on B, the critique doesn't apply and desk deviations are pure discretion. Everything downstream in the pipeline hedges with B.

**Desk vs quant.** The reactivity xcorr and trigger profile reverse-engineer the desk's implicit update rule; the timing-band table prices the EOD-stamp convention (one-signed with |t|>2 means update timing is itself a P&L factor — route it to slippage, don't bury it in the basis definition); PELT breaks on the desk−quant spread date shifts in discretionary behavior.

**Hedge-relationship stability — "when do ratios need to change."** Read in this order: Kalman innovation flags (|z|>3.5 clusters and the CUSUM-sq locator) are the sharp online tripwire — on the synthetic they date the planted duration break to the day; PELT on the beta path gives the dated mean shifts (a step shows as a ~60-day ramp because the production window smears it); sup-F and Nyblom bootstrap p-values are the formal test that the relationship is unstable at all (their argmax is only a coarse locator); the recursive CUSUM reading "never" is expected — it is slope-blind with a mean-zero regressor, not evidence of stability. Discard any beta break flagged as within 5 days of a Treasury OTR switch (hedge-leg DV01 step, not a TBA regime). A defensible declaration: two of {Kalman flags, PELT date, sup-F rejection} agreeing within a window, none DV01-flagged.

**Basis distribution.** PELT-rbf and the variance-specialist detector date distributional and vol shifts in the hedged basis. Critically, breaks labelled "just after a beta break" are hedge-adaptation leakage — the rolling ratio catching up — and should not be traded as basis regimes; fix the hedge instead.

**Vol-beta regime.** Instability in the basis' loading on dVol is the structurally-short-vol channel changing — typically the earliest confirmation in hiking-cycle-type episodes, since vol regimes flag in days while drift needs weeks.

**Drift regimes — your carry-up vs widening-down question.** The MS table gives per-state slope (points/day) and expected dwell; the filtered widening probability with the 0.8/0.2 hysteresis is the tradeable flag; the segment table gives the dated slope history. Latency honesty: a drift flip needs roughly (σ/Δμ)² ≈ 2–4 weeks of returns to confirm, so operationally the vol/covariate layer is the trigger and this layer the confirmation; slow QT-style widening with no vol spike is only caught here, mostly ex post. Then the attribution table answers "where from": `residual_demand` is the true spread/demand shock (the real basis regime); `convexity` is realized-variance bleed (a calm-but-choppy market flattens the basis with zero widening — don't misread it); `leakage` says the hedge was wrong, not the view; `carry` isolates roll/funding regimes; rally/selloff beta asymmetry is the flight-to-quality fingerprint (war/tariff-type risk-off). For known candidate dates, the events overlay is sharper than segmentation. The effective-episode line is binding discipline: post-2010 you have single-digit-to-low-double-digit widening episodes, and that — not row count — is your sample size for any regime claim.

**Forecast harness.** OOS R² and Clark–West answer "does the model beat the mean unconditionally"; Giacomini–White with the lagged regime instrument answers the claim a regime signal actually makes — "does the regime predict *when* the model wins." Expect GW to matter and CW not to; that pattern (visible in the synthetic run) is the honest signature of regime-conditional predictability. The economic table nets turnover costs, which is where whipsawing regime signals die. To test your stochastics: add your %D as a column in the feature block in `run.py` and it enters the same CW/GW/economic evaluation.

**Coupon swaps.** `basis_coupon_swaps.csv` holds b_c1 − b_c2 for adjacent liquid pairs — identically your duration-neutral swap P&L (tsy hedge = hr1−hr2); the ratio-weighted version is b_c1 − (hr1/hr2)·b_c2. Any layer above can be re-run on a swap column by pointing the reference series at it.

## 3b. Empirical calibration (what replaces the textbook constants)

Two thresholds are calibrated to your data rather than taken from a table, controlled by the `calibration` block in `config.py` (set either mode to `"fixed"` to revert).

**Shewhart flags on Kalman innovations.** You set one number — `shewhart_alarm_per_year`, the false-alarm rate you are willing to run. *Both* modes then derive their cutoff from it, so switching mode changes the estimator and never the operating point: `fixed` inverts the Gaussian (0.12/yr → |z| > 3.49, i.e. the conventional 3.5), `empirical` reads the same tail probability off your own innovations. The default 0.12/yr therefore reproduces the incumbent 3.5 convention exactly.

Reaching that tail empirically needs care. 0.12/yr is a per-day probability of 4.65e-4 — one day in 2150 — and a 500-day window has no order statistic out there at all. Asking for that quantile directly just returns the window maximum, which fires *far below* the design, not at it. So the empirical mode fits a Generalized Pareto to exceedances over the 95th percentile (~25 well-estimated points in 500 days) and extrapolates: standard peaks-over-threshold. If you dial the alarm rate up far enough that the window can read the tail off directly (`window × tail ≥ shewhart_min_exceed`), it uses the raw quantile instead and skips the model. Warm-up and any failed fit fall back to the Gaussian cutoff *for the same rate*, so the operating point is continuous. Everything is strictly trailing; the tail is refit every `shewhart_refit_every` days and held between refits.

**Read the cutoff, not the flag rate.** The realized flag rate is pinned near the design by construction in *either* mode — it is a readback of your config and carries no information about tail shape. The diagnostic is the **cutoff ratio**: at a matched tail probability, the level your data demands versus the level a Gaussian demands. The report calls ≥1.25x materially fat-tailed, ≥1.10x mildly so. Validated against known distributions: Gaussian draws give **1.01x**, Student-t(4) **1.91x**, Student-t(3) **2.51x** — and at t(3) a fixed 3.49 fires at 2.30/yr against its nominal 0.12/yr, which is the order-of-magnitude overshoot the constant costs you. The report prints that in-sample Gaussian-cutoff rate too; unlike the calibrated rate it is free to differ from the design, so it is informative.

**A near-1 ratio plus heavy over-firing means non-stationarity, not thin tails.** If the typical-window tail looks Gaussian yet the Gaussian cutoff over-fires several-fold, the two only reconcile if the innovation *scale* shifts between regimes — |z| well-behaved within a regime, clustered across regime changes. The report says so explicitly when it sees that pattern. Note the circularity it creates: a trailing threshold adapts *upward* during the very episode you want flagged. So treat clustered flags (two in five days) as the signal rather than the raw count, and lean on CUSUM-sq for the level shift itself. Cluster logic still applies on top.

**Point-in-time caveat.** The threshold is strictly trailing, but `z` is not: `kalman_tvp` estimates (r, q_a, q_b) by MLE over the whole sample, so the innovations being thresholded already embed future information. The *live* threshold is tradeable; the historical flag series is in-sample description. Making the whole history PIT would require expanding-window re-estimation of the Kalman parameters.

**PELT penalties.** `pen = c·log(T)` is a modelling convention carrying no error-rate guarantee, and it over-detects badly on persistent series: a rolling beta path has AR(1) ≈ 0.999, and a near-random walk genuinely wanders, so l2 segmentation finds "mean shifts" in pure noise. The empirical mode simulates the no-break null and picks the smallest penalty on the grid whose family-wise false-alarm rate meets the target (default 5%) — smallest because that maximizes power subject to the error constraint.

Everything then rests on how the no-break null is built (`pelt_null`), and all three options are wrong in different directions:

- `level` — block-resample the levels. Chunks are drawn from unrelated levels of a wandering series, so every seam is a genuine mean shift: the null arrives pre-loaded with exactly the breaks it exists to exclude, and the resulting "5%" is not a false-alarm rate.
- `difference` — resample increments and cumulate. Seam-free, but it imposes an *exact* unit root, replacing a mean-reverting series with a true random walk that wanders harder than the truth.
- `ar_sieve` — **the default.** Fit an AR(p) to the series and resample its residuals in blocks, so persistence is *estimated* rather than assumed in either direction.

In every case the residual/increment block must exceed the series' own overlap — a 60d rolling beta has MA(60) increments, hence `pelt_boot_block: 120`.

The sieve is what makes the calibration trustworthy in both directions. On simulated paths (n=1750, l2, `min_size=90`) it selects **pen=2 on iid noise** — i.e. it reproduces the textbook `c·log(T)` when the textbook is right — then **80 at AR(1)=0.98** and **320 at AR(1)=0.999**. A procedure that only ever inflated the penalty would be indistinguishable from a fudge factor; this one defers to the convention where the convention holds.

`pelt_boot_B` governs how finely the target can be resolved: the Monte-Carlo SE at a 5% target is √(.05·.95/B), so 1.5% at the default B=200 but 3.4% at B=40 — coarser than the target itself, and biased anti-conservative because the rule takes the *smallest* penalty that clears it. The report prints the achieved rate with its MC error. Cost is kept manageable by pruning: a penalty whose dirty count already exceeds target·B can never qualify, and by monotonicity neither can anything below it.

**Expect to lose power.** On the synthetic sample the honest penalty (320) is high enough that PELT no longer recovers the planted beta break at all — the break is still caught by sup-F and by the Kalman Shewhart/CUSUM-sq layers. That is the real cost of false-alarm control on a near-unit-root series, and it was previously hidden behind a null that was not valid. Treat PELT on the beta path as descriptive and let the other layers carry the detection.

Only the **beta path** is calibrated; the other PELT lines still use the uncalibrated `pelt_pen_scale`, and the report says so. If no penalty on the grid gets under target, the series is too persistent for segmentation to be inferential at all — its dates are descriptive only, lean on the Kalman layer, and widen `pelt_pen_grid` upward.

## 4. Monitoring workflow

Daily: append the new rows, rerun. Filtration-respecting statistics — safe to act on as they update — are the Kalman filtered beta and innovation flags, the MS and HMM *filtered* probabilities with hysteresis, and the rolling vol-beta. Full-sample statistics — PELT dates, sup-F, segments, smoothed probabilities, the attribution over closed episodes — are descriptive; re-read them weekly and expect the last segment's boundary to move as data arrives (end-of-sample instability is inherent to segmentation, not a bug). A practical alert set: Kalman |z|>3.5 twice in five days; CUSUM-sq drifting; MS filtered widening prob crossing 0.8; vol-beta rolling estimate leaving its 1-year range; a new PELT break on the desk−quant spread.

## 5. Reconciliation against your own series

Build the pipeline's daily basis increments (from `basis_quantB_by_coupon.csv` or the desk-hedged variant) and diff against day-over-day changes of your `Mid_dynamicAdj`-based close series for the same pair. Expect near-exact agreement; any residual localizes to a short list: hedge-ratio timing (bounded by the timing-band table), your outlier filter deleting days, roll-date set differences (48H−1b alignment), cutoff-time differences between legs, or a `TSY_ROLL_DF` gap measured asynchronously. Also diff `tba_backadjusted_levels.csv` against your adjusted `Mid` levels — they should match up to a constant per coupon. Reconciling once validates both stacks; after that, discrepancies are themselves data-quality alerts.

## 6. Failure modes

Duplicate (date,coupon) rows are deduped keep-last — make the export unique upstream. Coupons drift in and out of the liquid window as CC migrates; coverage-driven NaN stretches are correct behavior, not missing data. Mixing FN and G2 in one directory will corrupt the CC and curve fits — separate runs. If `tsy_yield` is absent, desk reactivity and the convexity attribution term are skipped. If the MS model degenerates (one state <2% frequency), history is too short or the studentization window needs widening — fall back to segmentation plus the vol trigger. And re-run `--synthetic` after any code modification: the 8/8 recovery table is the regression test that keeps every detector honest.
